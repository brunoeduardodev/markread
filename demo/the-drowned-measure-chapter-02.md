# Chapter Two: No Empty Shore

By sunrise, Rushwater had divided into those who were packing and those who had found reasons not to.

Asta stood above Halla’s flooded field and watched both groups work. Bedding, grain sacks, and frightened children moved uphill. Furniture did not. Beyond the chapel rise, the **moor** stretched northward—open, uncultivated country where wind bent the rough grass and no wall promised shelter.

Three nights, Derran had said.

It looked like time enough until Asta counted four hundred and twelve people.

Halla came from the **byre** leading the nine cows they had recovered. She had slept perhaps half an hour. Her face was **haggard**, grey with exhaustion, but her grip on the lead rope remained firm.

“The council is waiting,” she said.

“What do they want?”

“Several incompatible miracles. You may choose which to perform.”

She turned the cattle toward the chapel rise. The youngest cow kept looking back at the drowned pasture and calling for the two that had not returned. Asta had heard the same bewilderment in neighbors asking whether chairs, seed grain, or family records should be carried first. No answer could make three nights larger.

“Selk?” Asta asked.

“Awake. Quarrelsome. Therefore improving.”

The chapel had lost part of its **thatch**, the bundled straw that covered its roof. Rain came through in a steady line, so the council crowded against the north wall. Everyone else filled the yard or stood on the **sward** above the bank, listening through the open doors.

Wet bundles filled the rear benches. The families who wanted to leave had brought proof that they were ready. Those who meant to stay had brought tools. Between them sat people who had packed one bag and hated themselves for both the packing and the delay.

No one **shunned** the bell well now. People stared at Caul’s two guards beside it with the fixed attention given to a dangerous dog.

Asta entered with Lio behind her. In the **midst** of the council, surrounded on every side by angry neighbors, stood an empty chair.

“For the Measure House,” Jory said. “The reeve thought we should hear both sides.”

“The Measure House spoke last night,” Halla said. “It took my hay.”

The reeve, a narrow woman named Enna Rusk, struck the floor with her walking stick. “We will **heed** evidence, not injuries. Asta, show us what you found.”

Asta unfolded the transfer table across a bench. Seven red lines ran into the black mark of Rushwater. People leaned forward, but the figures meant nothing without Derran.

“It is a **wretched** choice,” Enna said, “but ignorance will not improve it.”

Lio spoke softly beside her. “Once you **wield** a truth—take it in hand and use it—you do not choose every cut it makes.”

“Is that advice from a mail skipper?”

“It is advice from someone who has delivered bad news and then been blamed for the handwriting.”

Asta looked toward the door. Derran waited behind the chapel, out of sight.

“Bring him,” she said.

Lio **beckoned** through the doorway. Derran entered under a storm of recognition. Several villagers had seen him carried from a ditch the previous week and believed him to be a penniless traveller. Halla blocked the first man who tried to seize him.

“Hear him,” Asta said. “Then decide what he deserves.”

Derran faced the council. In daylight he looked **gaunt**, so thin from hunger and strain that his cheekbones seemed ready to break the skin.

“I wrote the table,” he said. “I also chose Rushwater as a receiving shore.”

The chapel erupted.

Halla turned on him. “You told us the House chose.”

“The House approved it. The first calculation was mine.”

“Why?” Enna demanded.

Derran touched the three blue marks beside Rushwater’s name. “Because your bank, **weir**, and spill fields could survive three shares if they were cleared in advance. I planned an evacuation. First Sounder Roe changed three shares to seven when the storm estimate worsened.”

“Did you object?” Asta asked.

“In writing.”

“Did you warn us?”

Derran looked toward the wet bundles. “No. I revised the figures.”

That answer quieted the chapel more completely than any excuse could have done.

“Four hundred and twelve people **dwell** here,” Halla said. “We live here, whatever your calculation calls us.”

“I know.”

“You knew when you wrote it.”

Derran did not answer.

The anger his confession **kindled** moved through the room, quick and hot. Some called for the bell to be reversed at once. Others demanded every boat for an evacuation. Enna struck the floor until the noise fell.

Under the shouting, older resentment continued to **smoulder**. Rushwater had sent grain levies east during lean winters and received promises in return. Now people named each promise and the official who had made it.

“Can the table be false?” she asked Asta.

“The **wedge** taken from our tuning frame carries seven fresh cuts. I can test what they do.”

“Where?”

“At my workshop.”

A bell rang outside—not the buried Measure, but a small hand bell struck three times.

Bellwarden Caul appeared in the chapel door. Morning light sharpened his face. Behind him, riders were taking rudders and sails from the boats at the quay.

“The outlook is **bleak**,” he said, “but not hopeless. The House will move every resident of Rushwater to the central coast before the autumn tide.”

“And afterward?” Asta asked.

“You will be resettled.”

“Rushwater will be gone.”

“Rushwater is houses. Its people can survive.”

Halla’s hand closed around the **haft** of the knife at her belt. “Say that again while standing in my flooded field.”

Caul ignored her. “A junior sounder was murdered. His table and a receiver **wedge** are missing. Until they are recovered, the harbor is closed.”

He looked past Asta. “Lio Farrow. Attend me.”

Lio went still.

“You know him?” Asta asked.

“I carried House mail for two years.”

“On survey boats,” Caul said. “Your recall oath remains in force.”

Lio’s easy expression did not change, but his confidence seemed to **wane**. “That oath ended when the House ended my pay.”

“Come outside, or I will discuss the reason before your friends.”

Lio followed him.

When he returned, his red sail and bronze mail token had been seized. Caul had also stretched a chain across the harbor mouth.

“He will search every house **ere** noon,” Lio said. “Before then, we need proof strong enough to survive his version of the truth.”

“What reason?” Asta asked.

Lio glanced toward Caul. “I broke a House seal.”

“Why?”

“Four village names vanished from a route sheet between breakfast and supper. I wanted to know whether the villages had vanished with them.”

“What did you find?”

“Caul found me first.”

“Then I will decide what it means without your help.”

“You already do that with remarkable speed.”

Enna gave them until noon to prove the table. If they could not, the council would surrender it and accept the evacuation.

Asta led Derran and Lio to her **smithy**, the workshop beside the tide channel where she shaped iron and repaired the village gates. Two of Caul’s riders watched the front door, so they entered through the coal hatch behind the flooded **crofts**.

Water had swollen the hatch frame. Asta used a short axe to **hew** a gap through the soft wood, cutting until Lio could force the door inward. The blows sounded dangerously loud, but the riders remained at the street entrance.

Inside, the forge was cold. Asta **kindled** a smaller fire than usual and set Rushwater’s witness cup on the stone bench. The cup was no larger than her palm, cast from the same bronze as the submerged bell. Smiths used it to compare tones when they suspected a crack below.

“Water,” Derran said.

Lio filled the cup from a covered jar. Asta passed him the **bellows**, the leather device that forced air into the forge. He pumped until the coals brightened from red to yellow.

Derran laid the tuning **wedge** near the heat. “Warm, not glowing.”

“You have ordered enough from my forge,” Asta said. “Explain.”

“Heat wakes the bearing lines. Too much heat ruins them.”

As the bronze warmed, seven thin cuts darkened along its edge. Each ended in a mark from the table.

The marks were not scratches made in haste. Their edges curved with the minute regularity of careful work. Whoever altered Rushwater’s frame had prepared the **wedge** far from the village and brought it ready to fit.

Derran reached toward the witness cup, then stopped. “May I?”

Asta nodded.

He **beckoned** her closer and placed the **wedge** against the cup’s rim. “Strike the wide end once.”

Asta chose her lightest testing hammer. The tool had been **wrought** by her mother, shaped by hand until its face was almost perfectly flat. She struck.

The sound was small and pure.

Water climbed the inner wall of the cup and gathered at its center in a trembling peak.

Lio swore.

He ran one finger around the cup’s outer wall as if searching for a hidden thread. The bronze was dry. The water held its impossible shape until the last trace of sound faded; then it fell flat with a soft splash.

Derran reversed the **wedge**. “Again.”

This time Asta **wielded** the hammer with deliberate care, controlling it as precisely as she would a blade. The water leapt outward. Half spilled across the bench.

“Receiving,” Derran said, turning the **wedge** one way. “Casting,” he said, turning it back.

Asta stared at the wet stone. She could not **fathom** how a sliver of bronze commanded water, but she understood the proof. “If we reverse the frame, the seven shares go outward.”

“Back to the bells that sent them.”

“Then the House lied. Rushwater can be saved.”

“At four minutes’ notice.” Derran’s voice hardened. “The seven shores have been promised calm water. Their side gates will be shut. Their animals and children will remain below the banks. Send the shares back, and you repeat last night seven times.”

Halla stood in the coal hatch. None of them had heard her enter.

“How many people?” she asked.

“More than twenty thousand across the seven shores.”

“And if they know beforehand?”

Derran looked at her. “A warned shore can open fields, clear low houses, and accept part of its own water. Both bells must be tuned together.”

“It will still flood.”

“Yes.”

Halla looked toward the hill where Selk lay with his crushed ankle. The fury in her face did not vanish. It changed direction.

“Then we warn them,” she said.

Outside, villagers had begun to **seethe**, their contained anger moving and churning through the yard. Two riders caught Derran as he climbed from the coal hatch. He did not resist. Asta carried the witness cup to the low wall and repeated the test. The water gathered. She reversed the **wedge**. It sprang across Enna’s sleeve.

Enna asked to see it again. So did Jory. Asta repeated the two notes until every household speaker had watched water gather and scatter with the turn of the bronze. Disbelief did not vanish all at once; it lost one defense at a time.

Silence followed.

Then Caul said, “A convincing trick.”

He stood on the **sward** with four riders. Two more held Derran between them.

“Release him,” Asta said.

“First Sounder Roe **bade** me recover a stolen table and arrest the man who stole it. He ordered me to prevent an unlicensed reversal.”

Derran looked tired beyond fear.

Caul faced the villagers. “Ask your witness about Rillcross.”

Derran closed his eyes.

“Three years ago,” Caul continued, “he altered a transfer to spare his birthplace. The rejected water struck Rillcross without warning. Sixty-three people died.”

Halla’s face hardened. “Is that true?”

“Yes,” Derran said.

“You knew what reversal did.”

“I knew what the tables predicted. I told myself the figures were uncertain.”

Caul released him with a shove. “Derran Coil is the **bane** of Rillcross—the cause of its ruin. Now he offers the same mercy to you.”

The council’s certainty fractured, leaving anger on one side and frightened doubt on the other.

Asta wanted to strike Derran. Worse, she wanted the proof to remain true despite the man who brought it.

“You chose Rushwater,” she said. “Then you ran.”

“Per believed the table should be public. I believed him too late.”

“And now?”

“Now I tell Sarne Head what I did before I ask them to accept a single cup of water.”

Sarne Head was the nearest source shore and the first scheduled to cast. Three thousand people lived there.

Caul stepped between them. “The harbor is closed. You will go nowhere.”

Halla lifted the witness cup. “My lower **croft** is already ruined. Open its channel before the Great Bearing. Let it take water on purpose.”

Jory stared at her. “That field feeds your cattle.”

“Not this winter.”

One by one, other voices joined hers. An empty pasture. A warehouse cellar. The old salt yard. Not enough to receive seven shares, but a beginning made of named losses rather than an unnamed victim.

Caul listened. For one moment Asta thought he might yield.

“You cannot build a coastwide agreement in three days,” he said quietly.

“Then help us,” Asta said.

“I am helping the greatest number I can.”

He held out his hand for the **wedge**.

Asta slipped it inside her coat.

“You will **rue** this choice,” Caul said. “When the water comes, regret will be the last thing left to you.”

“Perhaps,” Asta said. “But it will be my regret, not one you assigned in secret.”

Enna stepped between them. “The council has not surrendered the table.”

“The table is House property,” Caul said.

“Rushwater is not.”

The reeve’s walking stick shook in her hand, but she did not move. Behind her, household speakers rose from the bench one by one. They had not agreed on evacuation, reversal, or resistance. They agreed only that Caul would not decide before they did.

Caul ordered his riders forward.

Jory overturned the witness bench into their path. Halla threw the cup to Lio. The yard broke into motion.

Asta **girded** the table case around her waist, fastening its strap tight so both hands remained free. Lio caught Derran by the sleeve. They ran behind the chapel and down through the flooded field.

Villagers closed around the riders without drawing a weapon. They asked questions, dropped baskets, led cattle across the lane, and otherwise **thwarted** pursuit with the stubborn innocence of people doing necessary work.

At the north spill, Lio’s mail boat waited without sail or rudder. Halla and six fishers had dragged it from the quay while the council argued.

“It cannot steer,” Asta said.

Lio took a long oar from beneath the seats. “It can disappoint expectations.”

Halla caught Asta’s arm. Up close she looked even more **haggard** than at dawn.

“You said you would not **forsake** Rushwater,” she said.

“I meant it.”

“Then go. Staying lets you defend one gate. Sarne may remove one whole share.”

“Caul will take the village apart looking for us.”

“He must find it first.” Halla pointed **yonder**, toward the chapel rise, where smoke had begun to pour from three chimneys. “Every family has moved to a different **hearth**. Let him count us.”

She pushed the boat into the spill channel.

“Cross at the old **ford**, the shallow river crossing, **ere** the tide covers it,” Jory called after them. “Before the water rises, or you will lose half a day.”

The channel carried them **thence**, from the sacrificed field toward the northern inlet. Behind them, Caul reached the bank but did not order his riders to shoot through the crowd.

He did something worse.

A blue flame **kindled** above the bell road.

Lio looked back once. “House signal. Receiver compromised.”

On the harbor side, a narrow cutter raised sail. It would take the clear sea road to Sarne and arrive before them.

Their choices had **dwindled** to one. They would beach below the northern **moor**, cross the open high ground on foot, and enter Sarne by the river **ford**—a shallow crossing that the rising tide would soon drown.

Without its sail, the mail boat crawled along the coast. Lio steered with the long oar while Asta emptied water from the bottom with the witness cup. Ahead, Caul’s cutter became a dark triangle against the pale sea. It was already more than halfway to Sarne.

Derran watched it without speaking.

“Will they listen to us?” Asta asked.

“Not after that cutter arrives.”

“That was not my question.”

“Then I do not know.”

By **gloaming**, the little boat reached the inlet. A **wayward** current, straying from the usual channel, tugged them west toward Rushwater. Lio fought it with the oar while Derran watched the first white water slide over the **ford** stones ahead.

Across the inlet, Sarne Head answered Caul’s signal with a blue light of its own.

“What does that mean?” Asta asked.

Lio’s face went still.

“They have been told we reversed our bell.”

“But we did not.”

“They do not know that.”

A deep note crossed the darkening water.

The current beneath the boat turned west.

Toward Rushwater.
