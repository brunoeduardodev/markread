# Chapter One: Seven Shares

The milk shivered before the bell rang.

At gloaming, in the dim blue hour after sunset, Asta Venn was kneeling beside a sick cow with one hand on the animal’s flank. No wind moved through the open door. Yet circles spread across the milk in Halla Norn’s pail, one after another, as if rain were falling indoors.

Asta felt the note rise through her knees.

It was too deep to hear at first. The iron nails in the wall trembled. A spoon crept across a shelf and fell. Then the cow bellowed, and the great bell beneath Rushwater’s tide channel gave a single bronze cry.

Halla dropped the pail. “That is not the storm call.”

“No.” Asta was already on her feet.

The other cattle began kicking at the stalls. Asta flung open the rear door of the byre, the low stone shelter where Halla kept twelve cows through winter. The animals crowded into the yard, wild-eyed but moving. Halla stared after them.

“The sea is half a mile out,” she said. “It cannot touch the bell at low water.”

Another vibration passed underfoot. This one bent the flame in the wall lamp toward the hill.

Asta ran.

Halla’s farm lay below the flood bank: a house, three fields, and the cattle shed. From its gate Asta could see the whole lower village. Rushwater occupied a shallow bowl facing the sea. Forty roofs lay inside the bank, with the tide channel cutting through the middle. Beyond them, boats rested on dark mud where the evening water had withdrawn.

People had come to their doors. They were looking toward the bell well, a round stone pit beside the channel gate. Every child in Rushwater knew to shun that place—to cross the road rather than go near it—because a bell heard at close range could stop the heart.

The well had no roof. In rain or snow, the opening remained bare to the sky, encircled by a wall too high for a child to climb. Asta had descended it only twice, both times with wax in her ears and a rope around her chest. Even at rest, the bronze below seemed to wait inside her bones.

The water in the channel began to run uphill.

For a moment Asta’s mind refused the sight. At low tide the channel should have been draining toward the distant sea. Instead a black tongue of water pushed inland, carrying weed, foam, and small fish. It struck the closed gate and climbed.

“Heed me!” Asta shouted. “Pay attention now. Open every lower pen and get the animals to the chapel rise.”

Halla caught her sleeve. “What is coming?”

“Water.”

“From where?”

Asta had no answer. She pulled free and sprinted up the bank.

The gate keeper, old Jory Pell, stood on the grass along the bank, turning in helpless circles. His two assistants were at supper. Asta seized his shoulders.

“Open the north spill gate.”

“The rule says keep all side gates shut during a bell sounding.”

“This is no lawful sounding.”

“Asta, I have held these gates since before you could walk.”

“Then you know the main one cannot bear that.”

The water struck. Timbers jumped in their sockets. A crack raced across the gate’s center brace.

Jory went white. Asta pointed past the mill. “The north gate is beyond the grain store. Run.”

He ran.

Asta slid down the inner bank to the gate platform. The weir—the low timber barrier that checked the tide channel—had begun to bow toward the village. Each pulse from the buried bell drove another mass of water against it. Not a wave, she realized. A weight. Someone was pouring the sea through a space too narrow to receive it.

She pulled the warning cord. Nothing happened at the beacon basket above the chapel.

“Wet fuel,” said a voice behind her. “Your watchman has the imagination of a boiled turnip.”

Lio Farrow jumped from the bank onto the platform. His mail boat stood strangely upright in the mud outside the gate, its red sail furled. He had lost his cap, and blood ran from a cut at his hairline.

“You came through that?” Asta asked.

“It came through me. What do you need?”

“Fire first.”

They climbed to the chapel basket. The watchman had packed it with damp straw. Lio tore open a mail sack, removed three official notices, and held them under the straw.

“Those carry the Measure House seal,” Asta said.

“Then they ought to burn with authority.”

She almost laughed. Together they kindled the beacon, setting the paper alight until flame caught the straw. Orange light sprang above the village. Doors opened. The chapel horn answered.

By the time they returned to the main gate, Jory had opened the north spill. Water spread through an empty pasture instead of hammering one point. The force on the main timbers eased, then rose again.

“There is another side gate,” Lio said.

“South, below Halla’s lower field.”

“If we open it, her house floods.”

“If we do not, the bank breaks and every house floods.”

The words cost Asta more than she let him see. Halla had delivered her at birth. Halla had fed her after her mother’s death and had never once asked repayment.

Lio watched her face. “Show me the release.”

They reached the south gate as water covered the path behind them. The lifting bar would not move. Its chain had twisted around the axle.

Asta snatched a splitting axe from the emergency rack. Her hands closed around the haft, the smooth wooden handle below the iron head. There was no room for a full swing.

“Hold the chain away from the wood,” she told Lio.

“That puts my fingers beside your axe.”

“Yes.”

“I am glad we understand each other.”

He pulled. She struck. Iron glanced from iron, showering sparks. On her third blow the chain opened. The gate rose half a hand, jammed, then tore upward as water burst under it.

The flood crossed Halla’s field in a shining sheet. Her croft—the small farm she worked as a tenant—lay directly in its path.

Asta saw Halla at the upper fence, waving a cloth. The cattle were climbing toward the chapel. The house might survive. The winter hay would not.

From the lower lane came a child’s scream.

Asta turned. A hand showed above the rushing water, vanished, then rose again beside a broken cart. Lio caught Asta’s arm before she jumped.

“There is a line,” he said.

He pointed to a heavy rope looped around the gate post. He tied it to Asta’s waist with a compact knot she had seen only on Measure House boats.

“Where did a mail skipper learn a court binding?” she asked.

“Where did a village smith learn to interrogate rescuers?”

“You can answer when I come back.”

He gave the rope one hard test, then opened his hand and beckoned, signalling her forward without wasting breath.

Asta entered the water.

It took her legs at once. The rope cut into her waist. She moved sideways, using the cart as a break, until she found not a child but Selk Norn, Halla’s sixteen-year-old nephew. His foot was trapped between two wheel spokes. Every breath was a cry.

“I cannot feel my toes,” he said.

“That is because the water is cold.”

“My ankle is broken.”

“You may complain about it on dry ground.”

The wheel had wedged beneath a roof beam swept from a storehouse. Asta could not lift it. She drew the small axe from her belt and began to hew through the beam, cutting it with fast, heavy blows. Lio paid out the rope as the water rose to her chest.

The cut widened, but the beam did not part. Asta struck again. Water lifted the cart, dropped it, and nearly took the axe from her. Selk stopped crying out. That silence frightened her more than his screams had done.

“Stay with me,” she said.

“I am considering other offers.”

“Refuse them.”

She set the blade into the cut and used the current itself, letting the floating cart wrench against the weakened wood. The beam split. Asta freed Selk’s leg, tied the line under his arms, and gave the signal. Lio pulled them both home.

Selk lay curled on the bank, shaking. His face had the pinched look of a much younger boy.

“This is wretched,” he whispered. “Cold, wet, crushed, and not even heroic.”

“You forgot loud,” Asta said.

His laugh became a sob. Halla pushed through the crowd and covered him with her coat.

Lio crouched to loosen the rescue line. His hands shook now that they had no urgent task. Asta noticed and chose not to mention it. Around them, neighbors formed a chain from the flooded houses to the upper bank, passing blankets, infants, sacks of meal, and one furious goose. Nobody had yet begun to ask who should pay for what had been saved.

They carried Selk to the sward, the stretch of close-cut grass on top of the bank. Halla knelt beside him while Asta checked that the bone had not broken through the skin. Around them, neighbors called names from house to house. Each answer loosened something in Asta’s chest.

Asta sat down hard on the wet ground.

Lio was studying the south gate. “You spread the burden.”

“I opened what I could.”

“Most keepers would have defended the main barrier until it broke.”

“Most keepers do not mend their own gates. Wood tells you when it is afraid.”

The beacon above the chapel had gone out. Damp straw continued to smoulder, burning slowly without flame and sending a thin ribbon of smoke into the dark.

Halla’s house still stood, though its door had been torn away. Two cows were missing. Barrels turned in the flooded lane, bumping softly against walls. Asta made herself count each loss because numbers were smaller than grief and easier to carry.

Jory returned with his assistants and reported that the north release was intact. The chapel rise held every child in the lower village. One fisherman had lost three fingers to a rope; no one else was badly hurt. It was a merciful account, if mercy could be measured against a disaster that had no right to exist.

Below them, the water began to wane. Its force diminished by degrees, first around their boots and then along the lane. The main gate remained standing. Water filled Halla’s lower field, the empty north pasture, and six cellars. It had not taken the village.

Men with lanterns searched the flooded lanes. Jory counted houses. Halla would not let anyone touch Selk until Asta examined his ankle. All the while, beneath the ordinary noise of rescue, the deep note continued in Asta’s bones.

It ended when someone shouted from the channel.

One of the fishers stood on the outer platform, pointing yonder—over the barrier and some distance along the channel—where a grey sleeve turned in the black water.

Two fishers were dragging a body from the outer face of the gate.

The dead man wore the grey coat of a junior sounder from the Measure House. He was perhaps twenty. One temple had been opened by a blow, but there was little blood in the wound. Water had carried him to Rushwater from somewhere else.

A leather case was strapped beneath his coat. Asta unbuckled it. Inside lay a sheet of waxed linen covered with ruled columns, tide figures, and small bell marks. Seven red lines ended at a black square labelled RUSHWATER.

Beside the chart was a wedge of bright bronze. Fresh scratches crossed its narrow edge. Asta knew the mark stamped on its head: the three reeds of Rushwater’s bell.

“That came from the tuning frame,” Jory said.

“It was removed today.” Asta touched the scratches. “He tried to put it back.”

Lio looked toward the sea road. “Then someone may have tried to stop him.”

Hooves sounded beyond the chapel.

Six riders entered the village wearing blue storm capes, though the sky was clear. Their leader, Bellwarden Caul, swung down beside the body. Asta knew him by sight. He inspected Rushwater every spring and drank only boiled water, even from sealed jars.

He should have been two days away.

“A calibration failed,” Caul announced. “The House regrets the damage.”

Halla rose from beside Selk. “You regret my hay?”

“Claims may be submitted at the winter session.”

“My cows will eat your claims, then.”

Caul ignored her. He crouched over the dead sounder and looked first at the man’s empty coat strap, then at Asta.

“He carried a transfer table,” he said. “Where is it?”

Lio answered before she could. “Gone through the gate.”

Caul’s eyes moved to the mail skipper. “You saw it?”

“I saw a leather case strike the barrier. It opened. Whatever was inside went thence—from there—out with the backwash.”

The lie was smooth. Asta held the folded chart under Selk’s discarded blanket and tried not to look at it.

Caul ordered his riders to search. They examined the gate, the lanes, and even the wet straw around the beacon. One tried to enter Halla’s house. She raised her milking stool with such purpose that he reconsidered.

The search did not uncover the chart. It did uncover anger.

People began to gather around the bellwardens, saying what they had lost. Caul listened with the patience of a locked door. At last he raised both hands.

“A test wave arrived at the wrong hour. No further danger exists. Go home.”

“What shore sent it?” Asta asked.

Silence passed among the blue capes.

Caul looked at her as if measuring the easiest place to cut. “No shore sends a wave. Storms make waves.”

“The sky is clear.”

“Then be grateful the test was small.”

He ordered the bell road closed and placed two men at the well. In doing so, he turned suspicion into certainty. Asta saw it move through her neighbors: not knowledge yet, but the recognition that knowledge was being kept from them.

Caul came close enough that only she and Lio could hear him.

“If either of you concealed House property, you did not thwart a search. You did not merely prevent my men from finding a paper. You endangered every shore in the Reach.”

“Your paper reached us before you did,” Asta said. “So did your dead man.”

For the first time, Caul’s expression changed. Not guilt. Fear.

He left without another word.

The village did not return to sleep. Families shifted wet bedding into upper rooms. Someone led the rescued cattle from the chapel yard and found places for them in dry pens. Asta reset the south release by lantern light, though the bent chain would need replacing. Work made the questions wait, but it did not make them smaller.

Lio followed her without speaking. Once, she caught him studying the Measure House knot still tied around the gate post. When he saw her looking, he turned away.

Near midnight Asta sat at Halla’s hearth, the stone floor before the fire, with the transfer table spread across her knees. Selk slept on the settle, his ankle bound. The cattle had been moved to the chapel yard. Rain tapped the shutters at last, gentle and useless.

Lio had remained. He claimed his boat needed a tide before it could leave, which was true, and that this was his only reason, which was not.

“Seven red lines,” Asta said. “Seven bells?”

“Seven shares,” said a stranger in the corner.

Lio reached for his knife. Asta took the fire iron.

The man stepped into the light with both hands raised. He was lean, unshaven, and haggard; sleeplessness had hollowed his eyes and drawn the skin tight across his face. Mud covered his coat to the shoulders.

“My name is Derran Coil,” he said. “I wrote that table.”

“Caul’s men searched this house.”

“They searched rooms. Halla hid me beneath the floor.”

From her chair beside Selk, Halla opened one eye. “He paid for soup.”

Asta kept the iron raised. “The dead sounder?”

“Per Venn. My assistant.” Derran’s voice caught on the name. “He took the table and the tuning wedge. He meant to warn you.”

“Warn us about a small test?”

“Tonight was a test. The first share lasted less than four minutes.”

Asta looked at the water mark halfway up Halla’s wall.

“What is a share?” Lio asked.

Derran moved toward the table. Asta blocked him.

“Explain from there.”

He pointed to the black square without touching it. “The bells cannot break a storm tide. They can only move its force. A tuned shore casts part of the water away. The table determines where that water arrives.”

Halla was fully awake now.

“Rushwater has a bell,” she said. “We cast our share elsewhere.”

“You did, once. Your frame was altered in spring.”

Asta remembered Caul drinking boiled water while his men worked alone below the bell well.

“Why us?” she asked.

Derran looked around the small room: damp walls, sleeping boy, boots lined beside the fire. “Because the central coast has eighty thousand people. Rushwater has four hundred and twelve.”

No one spoke.

The fire shifted. One wet log hissed.

“The autumn tide comes in three nights,” Derran continued. “The House expects the largest bearing in two centuries. Seven populous shores have been promised complete protection.”

Asta traced the red lines. They did not connect Rushwater to seven rescuers. They pointed like seven knives.

“I will not forsake this place,” she said. “I will not abandon it for your arithmetic.”

“Nor should you.”

“Then tell me how to turn the shares back.”

“That would send each one home.” Derran’s gaze went to Selk. “You would save four hundred people by surprising many thousands with water they were promised they would never see.”

Asta lowered the fire iron.

Until that moment she had thought the danger was a hidden weapon aimed at Rushwater. A weapon could be broken. An enemy could be named. But this was a weight already falling, and every hand that pushed it away chose another body to stand beneath it.

“There must be empty coast,” Lio said.

“Not enough. Not for what is coming.”

Outside, the buried bell gave a faint aftertone. Cups trembled on Halla’s shelf.

Derran looked at Asta.

“At the full bearing, seven shares will not flood your fields. They will remove this village from the map.”

“How long will we have?”

“Once the first bell sounds?”

He glanced toward the shutter, where the eastern sky was already losing its blackness.

“Until sunrise.”
