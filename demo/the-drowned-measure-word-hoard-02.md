# The Word-Hoard

> **moor** — *n., chiefly British; alive landscape term.* Open, uncultivated country, often treeless and covered in rough vegetation.  
> Etymology: Old English *mōr*, “waste ground, marsh.”  
> In this chapter: “Beyond the chapel rise, the **moor** stretched northward—open, uncultivated country where wind bent the rough grass and no wall promised shelter.”  
> Fresh example: The research station stood alone on a Yorkshire **moor**.  
> Register note: Ordinary in British geography and literary landscape description; less familiar elsewhere.  
> PT anchor: *charneca*; Portuguese *mouro* is a false friend.

> **thatch** — *n., alive rural/building term.* Straw, reeds, or similar plant material used to cover a roof.  
> Etymology: Old English *þæc*, “roof, covering.”  
> In this chapter: “The chapel had lost part of its **thatch**, the bundled straw that covered its roof.”  
> Fresh example: After the fire, volunteers replaced the cottage’s **thatch**.  
> Register note: Natural when discussing traditional buildings; also used in *thatched roof*.  
> PT anchor: *cobertura de palha*.

> **midst** — *n., literary/formal; alive chiefly in a fixed phrase.* The middle or central position.  
> Etymology: Middle English *middest*, from *mid* with an old superlative ending.  
> In this chapter: “In the **midst** of the council, surrounded on every side by angry neighbors, stood an empty chair.”  
> Fresh example: A technical failure arose in the **midst** of the broadcast.  
> Register note: Most natural in *in the **midst** of*; more formal than *in the middle of*.  
> PT anchor: *em meio a; no meio de*.

> **wield** — *v., alive; often literary or formal.* Hold and use a weapon or tool effectively; exercise power or influence.  
> Etymology: Old English *wealdan*, “rule, control.”  
> In this chapter: “Once you **wield** a truth—take it in hand and use it—you do not choose every cut it makes.”  
> Fresh example: The committee **wields** considerable influence over hiring.  
> Register note: Fully usable, especially with *power*, *authority*, *influence*, and weapons.  
> PT anchor: *empunhar; manejar; exercer*.

> **gaunt** — *adj., alive; somewhat literary.* Extremely thin and bony, often from hunger, illness, or prolonged strain.  
> Etymology: Late Middle English; its origin is uncertain.  
> In this chapter: “In daylight he looked **gaunt**, so thin from hunger and strain that his cheekbones seemed ready to break the skin.”  
> Fresh example: Months of illness left him **gaunt**.  
> Register note: Stronger than *thin* and usually suggests suffering or deprivation.  
> PT anchor: *esquálido; encovado*.

> **dwell** — *v., alive; literary/formal in the sense “reside.”* Live in a place; also remain mentally on a subject.  
> Etymology: Old English *dwellan*, originally “delay, lead astray,” and later “remain.”  
> In this chapter: “‘Four hundred and twelve people **dwell** here,’ Halla said. ‘We live here, whatever your calculation calls us.’”  
> Fresh example: The report does not **dwell** on minor errors.  
> Register note: *Dwell on* is common; using *dwell* for “live” sounds elevated or formal.  
> PT anchor: *morar; habitar; deter-se em*.

> **bleak** — *adj., alive.* Cold, bare, and exposed; figuratively, offering little hope.  
> Etymology: From Old Norse *bleikr*, “pale, whitish.”  
> In this chapter: “‘The outlook is **bleak**,’ he said, ‘but not hopeless.’”  
> Fresh example: After the funding was withdrawn, the project’s future looked **bleak**.  
> Register note: Fully usable in speech and writing for landscapes, weather, prospects, or moods.  
> PT anchor: *desolador; sombrio; pouco promissor*.

> **ere** — *prep. or conj., archaic/poetic.* Before; before something happens.  
> Etymology: Old English *ǣr*, “earlier, before.”  
> In this chapter: “‘He will search every house **ere** noon,’ Lio said. ‘Before then, we need proof strong enough to survive his version of the truth.’”  
> Fresh example: Finish the letter **ere** you leave.  
> Register note: A recognition word for historical, biblical, or poetic prose; avoid in ordinary modern speech.  
> PT anchor: *antes de; antes que*.

> **smithy** — *n., historical/literary.* A smith’s workshop.  
> Etymology: Old Norse *smiðja*, related to *smith*.  
> In this chapter: “Asta led Derran and Lio to her **smithy**, the workshop beside the tide channel where she shaped iron and repaired the village gates.”  
> Fresh example: The restored **smithy** opens to visitors on weekends.  
> Register note: Natural in historical or traditional-craft contexts; *forge* or *workshop* is more usual today.  
> PT anchor: *ferraria; oficina do ferreiro*.

> **bellows** — *n., craft term; grammatically singular or plural in form.* A device pumped to force air into a fire.  
> Etymology: Ultimately from Old English *belg*, “bag, skin.”  
> In this chapter: “Asta passed him the **bellows**, the leather device that forced air into the forge.”  
> Fresh example: A small **bellows** kept the jeweller’s flame hot.  
> Register note: Precise and current wherever fires, forges, organs, or traditional equipment are discussed.  
> PT anchor: *fole*.

> **wrought** — *adj. or old past participle; literary/historical.* Made, shaped, or brought about through work.  
> Etymology: Old English *worht* or *gewrōht*, from *wyrcan*, “work.”  
> In this chapter: “The tool had been **wrought** by her mother, shaped by hand until its face was almost perfectly flat.”  
> Fresh example: The balcony was enclosed by a **wrought**-iron rail.  
> Register note: Alive in *wrought iron* and elevated prose; not the normal modern past tense of *work*.  
> PT anchor: *trabalhado; forjado*.

> **fathom** — *v., alive; literary-neutral.* Understand something difficult or mysterious.  
> Etymology: From Old English *fæðm*, “outstretched arms”; first a depth measure, then a verb for understanding.  
> In this chapter: “She could not **fathom** how a sliver of bronze commanded water, but she understood the proof.”  
> Fresh example: I cannot **fathom** why the alarm failed twice.  
> Register note: Fully usable, usually when something is difficult to understand.  
> PT anchor: *entender a fundo*; the nautical noun corresponds to *braça*.

> **seethe** — *v., alive.* Boil or churn violently; figuratively, contain intense anger.  
> Etymology: Old English *sēoþan*, “boil.”  
> In this chapter: “Outside, villagers had begun to **seethe**, their contained anger moving and churning through the yard.”  
> Fresh example: He smiled politely while privately **seething**.  
> Register note: Natural in writing and speech, especially for strong anger that is not openly expressed.  
> PT anchor: *ferver; fervilhar*.

> **bade** — *v., archaic/literary past tense of* bid. Ordered, instructed, invited, or told.  
> Etymology: Old English *bēad*, past tense of *bēodan*, “offer, command.”  
> In this chapter: “First Sounder Roe **bade** me recover a stolen table and arrest the man who stole it. He ordered me to prevent an unlicensed reversal.”  
> Fresh example: The host **bade** us enter.  
> Register note: Primarily a recognition word for older or elevated prose.  
> PT anchor: *mandou; ordenou; convidou*.

> **bane** — *n., alive but literary.* A cause of ruin, destruction, or severe harm.  
> Etymology: Old English *bana*, “killer, slayer.”  
> In this chapter: “Derran Coil is the **bane** of Rillcross—the cause of its ruin.”  
> Fresh example: Unreliable batteries were the **bane** of the expedition.  
> Register note: Most natural in the still-current phrase *the **bane** of…*.  
> PT anchor: *ruína; perdição; flagelo*.

> **rue** — *v., alive but literary or old-fashioned.* Bitterly regret.  
> Etymology: Old English *hrēowan*, “grieve, make sorry.”  
> In this chapter: “‘You will **rue** this choice,’ Caul said. ‘When the water comes, regret will be the last thing left to you.’”  
> Fresh example: She would later **rue** the rushed decision.  
> Register note: Concise and expressive in literary writing; uncommon in casual conversation.  
> PT anchor: *arrepender-se; lamentar*.

> **gird** — *v., archaic/literary in literal use.* Fasten something around the body; encircle; prepare oneself for action.  
> Etymology: Old English *gyrdan*, “encircle, fasten.”  
> In this chapter: “Asta **girded** the table case around her waist, fastening its strap tight so both hands remained free.”  
> Fresh example: A stone wall **girds** the oldest part of the town.  
> Register note: Literal use is rare; *gird oneself for* remains available in formal or humorous English.  
> PT anchor: *cingir; prender ao redor*.

> **ford** — *n., alive landscape term.* A shallow place where a river or stream can be crossed.  
> Etymology: Old English *ford*, from the Germanic word for a crossing.  
> In this chapter: “‘Cross at the old **ford**, the shallow river crossing, **ere** the tide covers it,’ Jory called after them.”  
> Fresh example: The hikers removed their boots before entering the **ford**.  
> Register note: Current and precise; frequent in British place-names ending in *-ford*.  
> PT anchor: *vau*.

> **dwindle** — *v., alive.* Become gradually smaller, fewer, or weaker.  
> Etymology: An early modern frequentative form of obsolete *dwine*, from Old English *dwīnan*, “waste away.”  
> In this chapter: “Their choices had **dwindled** to one.”  
> Fresh example: Attendance **dwindled** as the evening progressed.  
> Register note: Fully usable; implies gradual reduction rather than a single sudden loss.  
> PT anchor: *minguar; diminuir aos poucos*.

> **wayward** — *adj., alive; literary.* Unpredictable, disobedient, or straying from the expected course.  
> Etymology: From Middle English *awayward*, “turned away.”  
> In this chapter: “A **wayward** current, straying from the usual channel, tugged them west toward Rushwater.”  
> Fresh example: The novel follows a **wayward** heir who refuses every family plan.  
> Register note: Natural in literary description; often applied to children, impulses, paths, or things that stray.  
> PT anchor: *rebelde; desgarrado*.
