# The Word-Hoard

> **gloaming** — *n., poetic/literary; alive but rare.* Twilight; the dim period after sunset.  
> Etymology: Old English *glōmung*, from *glōm*, “twilight”; kin to *glow*.  
> In this chapter: “At **gloaming**, in the dim blue hour after sunset, Asta Venn was kneeling beside a sick cow with one hand on the animal’s flank.”  
> Fresh example: The last train reached the mountain station at **gloaming**.  
> Register note: Excellent for atmospheric writing; likely to sound affected in ordinary speech.  
> PT anchor: *lusco-fusco*.

> **byre** — *n., regional/historical.* A shelter or shed in which cattle are kept.  
> Etymology: Old English *bȳre*, “cow stall, cattle shed.”  
> In this chapter: “Asta flung open the rear door of the **byre**, the low stone shelter where Halla kept twelve cows through winter.”  
> Fresh example: The museum converted the restored **byre** into a classroom.  
> Register note: Natural in British or Irish rural and historical settings; rare elsewhere.  
> PT anchor: *estábulo de vacas*.

> **shun** — *v., alive; literary-neutral.* Deliberately and persistently avoid someone or something.  
> Etymology: Old English *scunian*, “avoid, shrink from.”  
> In this chapter: “Every child in Rushwater knew to **shun** that place—to cross the road rather than go near it—because a bell heard at close range could stop the heart.”  
> Fresh example: After the privacy scandal, cautious customers **shunned** the app.  
> Register note: Fully usable; stronger than *avoid* because it suggests a conscious rejection.  
> PT anchor: *evitar deliberadamente*.

> **heed** — *v., alive; formal/literary.* Pay careful attention to or act upon a warning.  
> Etymology: Old English *hēdan*, “observe, guard, take care.”  
> In this chapter: "“**Heed** me!” Asta shouted. “Pay attention now. Open every lower pen and get the animals to the chapel rise.”"  
> Fresh example: He finally **heeded** the warning light on the dashboard.  
> Register note: Especially natural with *warning*, *advice*, and *words*; slightly formal in speech.  
> PT anchor: *dar ouvidos a; atentar para*.

> **weir** — *n., landscape/engineering; alive but specialist.* A low barrier across a river or channel that controls the water’s level or flow.  
> Etymology: Old English *wer*, “dam, fence placed in water.”  
> In this chapter: “The **weir**—the low timber barrier that checked the tide channel—had begun to bow toward the village.”  
> Fresh example: Engineers measured the flow immediately above the damaged **weir**.  
> Register note: Ordinary where river management or fishing is discussed; otherwise specialist.

> **kindle** — *v., alive; somewhat literary in figurative use.* Set something alight; also arouse a feeling or process.  
> Etymology: Middle English *kindlen*, from Old Norse *kynda*, “light a fire.”  
> In this chapter: “Together they **kindled** the beacon, setting the paper alight until flame caught the straw.”  
> Fresh example: The discovery **kindled** her interest in family history.  
> Register note: Natural in writing; *light* is more usual for a literal fire in casual speech.  
> PT anchor: *acender; despertar*.

> **haft** — *n., craft/weapon term.* The handle of an axe, knife, spear, or similar implement.  
> Etymology: Old English *hæft*, “handle.”  
> In this chapter: “Her hands closed around the **haft**, the smooth wooden handle below the iron head.”  
> Fresh example: The conservator replaced the knife’s cracked **haft** with ash wood.  
> Register note: Precise and useful when discussing traditional tools or weapons.  
> PT anchor: *cabo* de uma ferramenta.

> **croft** — *n., chiefly Scottish; regional/historical.* A small enclosed agricultural holding, often worked by a tenant.  
> Etymology: Old English *croft*, “small enclosed field”; its deeper origin is uncertain.  
> In this chapter: “Her **croft**—the small farm she worked as a tenant—lay directly in its path.”  
> Fresh example: The census recorded one abandoned **croft** above the quarry.  
> Register note: Not a general synonym for any farm; it carries a Scottish and smallholding association.

> **beckon** — *v., alive.* Signal with the head or hand for someone to approach or proceed; figuratively, attract.  
> Etymology: Old English *bēacnian*, “make a sign”; related to *beacon*.  
> In this chapter: “He gave the rope one hard test, then opened his hand and **beckoned**, signalling her forward without wasting breath.”  
> Fresh example: A green light **beckoned** passengers toward the empty platform.  
> Register note: Fully usable in speech and writing.  
> PT anchor: *chamar com um gesto; acenar para que alguém venha*.

> **hew** — *v., literary or traditional craft usage.* Cut, chop, or shape with heavy blows.  
> Etymology: Old English *hēawan*, “strike, chop, cut.”  
> In this chapter: “She drew the small axe from her belt and began to **hew** through the beam, cutting it with fast, heavy blows.”  
> Fresh example: The rescuers **hewed** steps into the ice.  
> Register note: More forceful and traditional than *cut*; common in historical prose.  
> PT anchor: *talhar; cortar a golpes*.

> **wretched** — *adj., alive; emphatic/literary.* Miserable, deeply unhappy, or in a very poor condition.  
> Etymology: From *wretch*, Old English *wrecca*, “exile, outcast, miserable person.”  
> In this chapter: "“This is **wretched**,” he whispered. “Cold, wet, crushed, and not even heroic.”"  
> Fresh example: The headphones produced a **wretched** hiss during every call.  
> Register note: Usable in speech, but emotionally stronger than *bad* or *unpleasant*.  
> PT anchor: *miserável; péssimo*.

> **sward** — *n., literary.* An expanse or surface layer of short grass.  
> Etymology: Old English *sweard*, originally “skin, rind,” and later the surface turf.  
> In this chapter: “They carried Selk to the **sward**, the stretch of close-cut grass on top of the bank.”  
> Fresh example: White chairs stood in rows across the wedding **sward**.  
> Register note: Atmospheric and precise in literary description; unusual in conversation.  
> PT anchor: *gramado*.

> **smoulder** — *v., alive; British spelling.* Burn slowly with smoke but little or no flame; figuratively, remain suppressed but active.  
> Etymology: Middle English *smolderen*; probably Germanic, though its deeper origin is uncertain.  
> In this chapter: “Damp straw continued to **smoulder**, burning slowly without flame and sending a thin ribbon of smoke into the dark.”  
> Fresh example: Resentment **smouldered** through months of polite meetings.  
> Register note: Natural in writing and British English; American spelling is *smolder*.  
> PT anchor: *arder em brasas*.

> **wane** — *v., alive; literary-neutral.* Decrease gradually in strength, size, influence, or intensity.  
> Etymology: Old English *wanian*, “lessen, diminish.”  
> In this chapter: “Below them, the water began to **wane**. Its force diminished by degrees, first around their boots and then along the lane.”  
> Fresh example: Her enthusiasm **waned** after the third postponement.  
> Register note: Natural in writing, especially of influence, confidence, light, and the moon.  
> PT anchor: *minguar; minguante*.

> **yonder** — *adv. or determiner, poetic/archaic/regional.* At or toward a place some distance away; over there.  
> Etymology: Middle English *yonder*, ultimately from Old English *geond*, “beyond.”  
> In this chapter: “One of the fishers stood on the outer platform, pointing **yonder**—over the barrier and some distance along the channel—where a grey sleeve turned in the black water.”  
> Fresh example: The guide pointed to a radio mast **yonder** on the ridge.  
> Register note: Literary, playful, or regional; normally affected in modern urban speech.  
> PT anchor: *acolá*.

> **wedge** — *n., alive; craft/material term.* A piece that narrows to an edge and is driven between objects to split, lift, tighten, or secure them.  
> Etymology: Old English *wecg*, “wedge.”  
> In this chapter: “Beside the chart was a **wedge** of bright bronze, thick at one end and tapering to an edge meant to be driven into a narrow gap.”  
> Fresh example: She folded the menu and used it as a **wedge** beneath the uneven table leg.  
> Register note: Fully usable; also common figuratively for something that forces people apart.  
> PT anchor: *cunha*.

> **thence** — *adv., archaic or formal.* From that place, time, fact, or source; from there.  
> Etymology: Middle English *thennes*, formed from *thenne* with an adverbial *-s*.  
> In this chapter: “Whatever was inside went **thence**—from there—out with the backwash.”  
> Fresh example: The samples went to the regional laboratory and **thence** to the national archive.  
> Register note: Acceptable in very formal writing; archaic in ordinary narration or speech.  
> PT anchor: *dali; de lá*.

> **thwart** — *v., alive; formal/literary.* Prevent a plan, effort, or person from succeeding.  
> Etymology: From Old Norse *þvert*, “across”; the verb developed from the idea of placing something across another’s course.  
> In this chapter: “If either of you concealed House property, you did not **thwart** a search. You did not merely prevent my men from finding a paper. You endangered every shore in the Reach.”  
> Fresh example: A missing permit **thwarted** the launch.  
> Register note: Fully usable, especially in writing; stronger and more decisive than *hinder*.  
> PT anchor: *impedir; frustrar*.

> **hearth** — *n., alive; literary in figurative use.* The stone or floor before a fireplace; by extension, the home and its domestic life.  
> Etymology: Old English *heorþ*, from a Germanic word for a fireplace.  
> In this chapter: “Near midnight Asta sat at Halla’s **hearth**, the stone floor before the fire, with the transfer table spread across her knees.”  
> Fresh example: The inspector found a crack running beneath the old **hearth**.  
> Register note: Literal use remains natural where fireplaces exist; the “home” sense is literary.  
> PT anchor: *lareira; lar*.

> **haggard** — *adj., alive.* Looking exhausted and unwell, especially with hollow eyes or a drawn face.  
> Etymology: From French *hagard*, “wild, untamed”; first used in English of an adult hawk caught in the wild.  
> In this chapter: “He was lean, unshaven, and **haggard**; sleeplessness had hollowed his eyes and drawn the skin tight across his face.”  
> Fresh example: After the overnight flight, the entire team looked **haggard**.  
> Register note: Natural descriptive English, though stronger than *tired*.  
> PT anchor: *abatido; com aspecto exausto*.

> **forsake** — *v., literary, archaic, or solemn.* Abandon, desert, or renounce.  
> Etymology: Old English *forsacan*, “reject, refuse, renounce.”  
> In this chapter: "“I will not **forsake** this place,” she said. “I will not abandon it for your arithmetic.”"  
> Fresh example: The committee refused to **forsake** the principles in its charter.  
> Register note: Solemn and often biblical; best reserved for serious abandonment.  
> PT anchor: *desamparar; abandonar*.
